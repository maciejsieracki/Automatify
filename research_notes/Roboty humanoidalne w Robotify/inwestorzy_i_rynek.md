## 1. Funding rounds, valuations and capital flows 2024–2026 (and why humanoids attract investors)

### Takeaway
Capital into humanoids and embodied AI rose steeply. PitchBook counted about $4.6B (~€4.0B) for humanoids in 2025, roughly 3× 2024, and Dealroom counts about $11B (~€9.5B) for 2026 year-to-date. That money goes overwhelmingly to a few hardware and "robot brain" companies with 10-figure valuations and little revenue: Figure $39B, Skild >$14B, Physical Intelligence $5.6B (talks at >$11B), Apptronik ~$5–5.5B, NEURA ~€4B–$7B. Strategic corporates (Nvidia, Amazon/Bezos, Google, SoftBank, Foxconn, Mercedes, Schaeffler/Bosch, Tencent, Tether) sit in almost every large round. Public markets confirmed the appetite but also its volatility: Unitree's August 2026 STAR IPO rose 487% on day one, then fell about 55% from its peak.

### Inferences
- The humanoid capital boom is concentrated in **platform plays**: hardware OEMs and foundation-model companies. They are priced on option value and strategic interest, not on revenue (Skild is reportedly ~467× run-rate revenue; see section 3). A deployer like Robotify cannot borrow these multiples. It can borrow the **narrative tailwind**: investors already accept that humanoids are coming and that someone will have to deploy, finance and maintain them on factory floors.
- Strategic corporates (automotive suppliers such as Schaeffler and Bosch, OEMs such as Mercedes, Hyundai and BMW, logistics operators such as GXO and Amazon) are the de facto first customers and investors. For a CEE deployer, partnerships with European humanoid makers (NEURA, Humanoid UK, Generative Bionics, Agile/RobCo) and with large Tier-1 suppliers present in Poland are the credible bridge to this capital pool.
- The European humanoid sector is now investable at scale: NEURA up to $1.4B, Humanoid UK $1.35B valuation, RobCo $1B, Generative Bionics €70M. A European/Polish deployer pitch can therefore cite European makers, not only US and Chinese ones.

## 2. Market size forecasts, their assumptions, unit shipments and where value is captured

### Takeaway
Realised volumes are still small. Roughly 13,000–18,000 humanoids shipped globally in 2025, more than 80% from Chinese makers, and 2026 forecasts range from about 16,000 to 75,000 units. Bank forecasts keep moving up: Goldman Sachs in September 2026 raised 2035 to ~6.5M units and ~$138B (~€119B). The forecasts span more than an order of magnitude because they rest on assumptions about cost (BOM falling from $30–150k to under $20k), autonomy and dexterity. Most published value is attributed to hardware units. Software, data and services are a thin layer in bank TAMs, and integration appears as a precondition for adoption rather than as a priced pool.

### Inferences
- For a seed pitch it is more defensible to quote a **range with named sources and dates**, e.g. "2035: $15B (Interact, conservative) to $138B (Goldman Sachs, Sep 2026)", than a single trillion-dollar figure. Trillion-dollar figures (MS 2050, Citi 2050, BofA 2060) are 25–35-year scenarios, and sophisticated investors discount them.
- Industrial humanoid volumes in Europe will be small through ~2030: global shipments are in the tens of thousands, mostly Chinese, and mostly not yet in productive factory work. This **supports the "cobots now, humanoids later" sequencing**. Robotify's revenue in 2026–2029 will realistically come from cobots and AMRs, with humanoids as a pilot and option line.
- Rapid hardware price deflation (−72% average unit price at Unitree in two years) has two sides for a rental fleet owner. It improves customer ROI, but it destroys **residual value** of owned units, so short depreciation schedules, vendor buy-back or operating leases matter.
- No bank TAM explicitly sizes "integration/RaaS for humanoids". McKinsey lists integration capabilities as a *precondition* of its base case, so the deployer layer is a bottleneck that is not separately priced. That can be framed as an opportunity, but it is an inference, not a sourced number.

## 3. Investor sentiment and risks: bubble concerns, sceptics, failures, Chinese competition, tariffs and export controls

### Takeaway
Sentiment is euphoric in private and public markets, but by autumn 2026 there were clear signs of a correction. Unitree fell ~55% from its post-IPO peak. Chinese regulators informally slowed humanoid IPOs (Reuters, 21 Sep 2026) and are scrutinising revenue from government-linked "data collection centres". Rental prices for humanoids in China collapsed by more than 10×, and NDRC had already warned in 2025 of a bubble with more than 150 makers. Well-known sceptics such as Rodney Brooks call it "a classic bubble". Weaker players have failed or pivoted (K-Scale Labs, Sanctuary AI). The US barred new foreign-made humanoids (FCC rule, July 2026), which splits supply chains.

### Cited Findings

**Bubble warnings and sceptics**
- Rodney Brooks (iRobot co-founder, Sep 2025 essay and interviews): "We are in a humanoid robot bubble". Billions go into experiments with little chance of economical mass production. Learning dexterity from human video is "pure fantasy". The human hand has ~17,000 tactile sensors. Successful "humanoids" in 15 years will likely have wheels and multiple arms. He calls the demos "humanoid theater" and the market "a classic bubble" — [TechCrunch, 10 Oct 2025](https://techcrunch.com/2025/10/10/the-world-is-just-not-quite-ready-for-humanoids-yet/); [Yahoo Finance](https://finance.yahoo.com/news/why-irobot-co-founder-thinks-145654542.html); [Forbes, 31 Dec 2025](https://www.forbes.com/sites/annatong/2025/12/31/2025-another-year-of-humanoid-hype/)

### Inferences
- In autumn 2026, investors are moving from "story" to **"deployment, volumes and valuation"** (Reuters banker quote). This favours a pitch built on contracted hours and paying customers over one built on humanoid visuals. It may also make humanoid-heavy pitches *less* convincing to experienced robotics VCs, so the owner's belief that humanoids are "the single most important element" needs qualifying.
- The Chinese rental-price collapse shows that **robot rental without productive use cases becomes a commodity**. Event and showpiece rentals crashed. A RaaS model tied to measurable production tasks (pallets moved, parts picked, OEE gains) is a different category, and the pitch must make that distinction explicit.
- Geopolitics creates both risk and a niche for a European deployer. Chinese humanoids are by far the cheapest and most available, but US rules already exclude new imports, and customers with US exposure or defence links may avoid Chinese hardware. A **vendor-agnostic deployer** that can offer EU or US-allied hardware (NEURA, Humanoid UK, Agility, Apptronik) and Chinese hardware where acceptable hedges this. Investors will ask which vendors Robotify plans to use.
- The failure modes so far (K-Scale undercut by Unitree; Sanctuary running out of runway) are **manufacturer** failure modes: capex-heavy R&D and price competition. A deployer avoids R&D burn but inherits **vendor risk**: the maker it standardises on may fail, lose support, or be blocked by regulation.

- No data on tariffs specifically applied to humanoid robots (US or EU).

## 4. Deployer, integrator and RaaS plays: examples, and how investors value them

### Takeaway
The most investable "non-maker" or hybrid models so far are RaaS and integration platforms for SMEs and logistics: Formic (US, pay-per-hour, debt-financed fleet), RobCo (Germany, RaaS with zero upfront investment, unicorn in Oct 2026), GXO (logistics operator renting Agility Digits), and in China maker-run rental arms (AgiBot's SHAREBOT) and dedicated rental exchanges (Qingtianzu, seed round Jan 2026). Investors value these on recurring-revenue logic (ARR, MRR per robot, gross margin, retention, payback) and fund the fleets with debt, not equity. Valuations are far below humanoid OEMs: Formic is ~$100–250M versus Figure at $39B. Public benchmarks for RaaS multiples are thin.

### Cited Findings

**Examples**
- **Formic (US)** is a pure deployer/RaaS operator for manufacturers. It owns, programs, installs and maintains robots, and customers **pay by the hour only after the robots are installed and fully functional**. Series A was $26.5M (~€22.8M; Jan 2022; Lux Capital lead) at a valuation of >$100M, plus **access to >$100M (~€86M) of debt capital to fund equipment**. A follow-on $27.4M (~€23.6M) came from Blackhorn Ventures with NEC and **Mitsubishi HC Capital America** (a leasing company) participating, bringing the Series A to >$52M. Dealroom valuation band: $100–250M (2024). Total funding is reported very differently ($59M Tracxn vs $213M Startupintros, likely incl. debt) — [The Robot Report](https://www.therobotreport.com/formic-brings-in-26-5-million-in-series-a-funding/); [Pulse 2.0](https://pulse2.com/formic-robots-as-a-service-automation-company-closes-27-4-million/); [Dealroom](https://dealroom.co/companies/formic-1/); [Tracxn](https://tracxn.com/d/companies/formic/__kIGIitoSsqOBd_l9LSQvh5VA_W6oiy82uH7CCEFZunQ)

## 5. What makes a humanoid narrative credible in an early-stage pitch, and what red flags investors see

### Takeaway
By late 2026, credibility comes from **evidence of productive deployment**: paid RaaS contracts with named industrial customers, measured output (totes moved, operating hours), strategic partners with offtake or production commitments, a financed fleet plan and a realistic timeline. Red flags are visible in the 2025–2026 shake-out: demo-driven "humanoid theater", revenue from subsidised or government-linked data centres, pre-orders that never convert, showpiece rentals with idle robots, single-vendor dependence on hardware that can be undercut or regulated away, and valuations unsupported by revenue.

### Cited Findings

**Credibility signals seen in funded companies**
- Paid, multi-year RaaS contracts with named logistics/industrial customers plus measurable output: Agility at GXO (>100,000 totes; RaaS contracts with GXO, Toyota, Mercado Libre, Schaeffler; >65,000 operating hours claimed) — [Agility](https://www.agilityrobotics.com/content/digit-moves-over-100k-totes); [newmarketpitch](https://newmarketpitch.com/blogs/news/humanoid-robotics-digit-deployment-tracker)
- Strategic industrial partners committing offtake or production: Humanoid UK (Schaeffler offtake, Bosch production capacity, both investors) — [Trending Topics](https://www.trendingtopics.eu/new-unicorn-uk-robotics-startup-humanoid-raises-e133-million-at-e1-1-billion-valuation/)
- Customer-backed deployment timelines with an on-site application/training centre: Hyundai/Boston Dynamics RMAC at Metaplant America, starting with "processes with proven safety and quality benefits, such as parts sequencing" and a 2028 deployment date — [The Robot Report](https://www.therobotreport.com/boston-dynamics-opens-metaplant-application-center-train-atlas-humanoid-robots/); [Hyundai](https://www.hyundai.com/worldwide/en/newsroom/detail/0000001100)

### Inferences (applied to Robotify; these are my reasoning, not sourced facts)
- **Position humanoids as the "option" and the long-term TAM, and cobots/RaaS as the engine.** A credible seed pitch shows (a) cobot RaaS revenue or LOIs now, priced per robot-hour; (b) a vendor-agnostic deployment platform (application engineering, safety integration, maintenance, fleet software, financing) that makes adding humanoids an upgrade of the existing service; (c) one or two humanoid pilots with named maker partners and named customers, with defined KPIs.
- **Evidence an investor will want:** signed pilot or LOI with a mid-sized manufacturer; written partnership or distribution status with at least one humanoid maker (ideally EU-based: NEURA, Humanoid UK, Generative Bionics; or Agility/Apptronik with EU presence); a safety and certification path (CE marking or risk assessment for mobile manipulators; see gaps); a fleet-financing plan (leasing partner or debt facility on the Formic and Mitsubishi HC Capital pattern); utilisation and unit-economics model per robot, incl. the residual-value risk from fast price deflation.
- **What not to claim:** do not imply manufacturer-like valuations; do not use trillion-dollar 2050 TAMs as the headline; do not present showpiece or event rentals as traction; do not rely on a single Chinese vendor without a plan for regulatory or reputational risk.
- **The owner's belief:** the evidence says humanoids are the most *attention-grabbing* element and do open doors to embodied-AI-themed funds and corporates. But in October 2026 investors explicitly reward **deployment proof and recurring revenue**, and humanoid narratives without them are being discounted (Unitree −55%, the CSRC slowdown, Chinese valuation cuts, Brooks's critique). Humanoids should be the *vision slide and roadmap*, not the *core valuation argument*.
