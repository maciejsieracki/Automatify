## 1. Documented deployments and paid pilots: what, where, how many, what results

### Takeaway
As of October 2026, only two enterprise humanoid deployments have publicly corroborated operating results. The first is Figure 02 at BMW Spartanburg: about 11 months, 1,250+ hours, 90,000+ parts. The second is Agility Digit at GXO: 100,000+ totes. Every other deployment (Apptronik at Mercedes, Atlas at Hyundai, Optimus at Tesla, UBTech in Chinese car plants, Hexagon AEON at BMW Leipzig, Humanoid/NEURA at Schaeffler) involves a single-digit to low-double-digit number of units, is in pilot, validation or "training" stage, and has no published KPIs on cycle time, interventions or productivity versus a human. Big numbers such as Hyundai's 25,000 Atlas units, Schaeffler's "thousands" and Tesla's 1M/yr are targets and framework agreements, not robots on the floor. In Europe, the real sites are BMW Leipzig (DE), Mercedes Berlin-Marienfelde (DE) and Kecskemét (HU), and Schaeffler Herzogenaurach/Schweinfurt (DE, from late 2026). I found no confirmed humanoid pilot in a Polish factory and no Nordic factory data.

## 2. What still limits humanoids in real factories (quantified where possible)

### Takeaway
The binding constraints in 2026 are:
- **Productivity:** reported at ~20% of a human in early trials. Interact's baseline assumes only ~50% of human productivity by 2032.
- **Runtime:** typically 2–4 h per charge against 8–12 h shifts, solved today by battery swaps or charging gaps.
- **Cost:** McKinsey puts it at $150k–500k per unit today and says it needs to reach $20k–50k.
- **Generalisation:** each new task needs retraining.
- **Safety certification:** ISO 25785-1 is still a draft, so most deployments are caged or segregated.

Reliability (MTBF) and teleoperation share are not disclosed by any vendor, which is itself a red flag.

### Inferences
- For a mid-sized manufacturer the limits stack up. One robot on a 2–4 h battery working at 20–50% of human speed means roughly 2–5 robots, or swap infrastructure, to replace one worker-shift on a simple handling task. At $100k+ per unit plus integration, that does not pay back unless the robot runs multiple shifts at high utilization, which is what IDTechEx's "six-month payback" scenario assumes.
- Safety is a gating item for Europe. Without a published ISO 25785-1 and harmonised EU Machinery Regulation conformity paths, fenceless humanoid work next to staff will rest on case-by-case risk assessments. The new EU Machinery Regulation 2023/1230 applies from January 2027; this is general knowledge, not verified in this session. Many sites will therefore keep humanoids in segregated zones, which removes much of the "drop-in for a human" advantage.
- Missing disclosure (no MTBF, no intervention rates, no cycle times) after ~2 years of high-profile pilots is evidence that the numbers are not yet favourable enough to publish.

**Testing the owner's assumptions (humanoids capable of real factory work by ~2028; cobot+humanoid mix 2028–2030; humanoids only after ~2030):**
- **Assumption 1 ("real factory work by ~2028"): partly supported, but only in a narrow sense.**
  - Supported: by 2027–2028, humanoids (increasingly wheeled) will plausibly do *productive, paid* work on narrow, repetitive, logistics-adjacent tasks such as tote and KLT handling, parts sequencing, kitting and simple machine loading. This will happen at large OEMs and Tier-1s (BMW, Hyundai, Schaeffler, Toyota, Mercedes) with vendor support, mostly through RaaS.
  - Not supported: "real factory work" in the broad sense of assembly, varied tasks, fenceless operation and full shifts at a mid-sized manufacturer. Gartner (<20 companies in production by 2028), Hyundai's own plan (assembly only from 2030) and Interact (inflection 2032) all argue against it.
  - Adjusted date: 2028–2030 for first viable *narrow* use cases at mid-sized plants (RaaS, 1–5 units, material flow). 2031–2033 for broader economic viability, once unit prices reach ~$30–50k, runtime covers a shift with swaps, ISO 25785-1 is published and in use, and productivity reaches ≥50% of a human.
- **Assumption 2 ("mixed cobot+humanoid period to ~2030"): reasonable as a phase, but too short.** The evidence points to a long hybrid period lasting well into the 2030s. Cobots, AMRs and mobile manipulators keep the stable-station work; humanoids fill flexible material-flow and exception tasks.
- **Assumption 3 ("after ~2030 only humanoids"): not supported by any source found.**
  - IFR says humanoids will not replace existing robot types.
  - Gartner recommends polyfunctional (non-humanoid) robots for most companies.
  - KUKA argues the humanoid form is unnecessary for most industrial applications.
  - Morgan Stanley's long-run view has adoption slow until the mid-2030s.
  - Even Goldman's bullish ~890k units in 2030 are global units across all uses, a fraction of which would be factory work.
  - The economics also argue against it: a fixed cobot is cheaper, faster and more precise at a stationary task than a general-purpose biped.
- **Where forecasts disagree:** the bulls (Goldman, BofA, IDTechEx's ROI framing) model volume and price curves and assume AI improves. The bears (Gartner, Interact, Brooks, Unitree's founder) focus on effective output per hour, generalisation and safety. Volumes and dollar figures also measure different things: global versus China-only, all uses versus productive work. Shipment counts are dominated by Chinese units for research, education and entertainment.

## 4. Cobots and humanoids together; humanoids replacing cobot cells; which tasks move first

### Takeaway
I found no documented case of humanoids *replacing* an existing cobot cell in production. The hybrid evidence is limited to vendor demonstrations, such as JAKA's two-humanoid engine cell next to its new 50 kg cobot at CIIF 2026, and to analysts' framing. That framing has cobots keeping stable, high-precision stations, while humanoids (or wheeled mobile manipulators) take over material flow between stations: totes, KLT boxes, sequencing and kitting, cell replenishment and exception handling. These are tasks done today by people, carts and AMRs, not by cobots. Several major automation players (KUKA, Gartner's recommendation, Brooks) argue that non-humanoid mobile manipulators will win many of these tasks anyway.

### Inferences
- For a mid-sized manufacturer, the evidence supports a layered architecture for the late 2020s:
  - Fixed stations: cobots (welding, screwdriving, machine tending, palletizing).
  - Material flow: AMRs or mobile manipulators.
  - Humanoids: a RaaS add-on for flexible handling tasks that span stations and resist fixed automation (sequencing from mixed bins, KLT handling to and from lines, replenishing cobot cells, exception handling).
  
  Humanoids are more likely to *feed and tend* cobot cells than to replace them.
- Tasks that would migrate from *cobots* to humanoids are few. Candidates are only low-volume, high-mix stations where re-fixturing a cobot for each variant costs more than a slower but more general humanoid. Even there, the 2026 evidence (each new task needs retraining, per Unitree) means the "general" advantage is not yet realised.
- A practical integrator positioning (Robotify): build cobot and AMR competence and a standard integration layer (WMS/ERP task dispatch, safety assessment, KPI telemetry). That same layer is what humanoid deployments need (see the SAP–Humanoid PoC). Then add humanoids as a module once vendors publish intervention, uptime and cycle-time data under contract.

### Gaps
- No production case found of a humanoid working *inside* or *directly alongside* a cobot cell with published KPIs, and no case of a cobot cell being decommissioned in favour of a humanoid.
- No data on integrator margins or on the practicalities of humanoid RaaS contracts in Europe.
