## 1. Sector-by-sector map: what humanoids (and precursor mobile manipulators) do today, and when each sector becomes realistic

### Takeaway
As of mid-2026, paid, multi-week humanoid deployments exist almost only in **structured industrial and logistics settings**: automotive plants, contract-logistics warehouses, electronics and EV factories, mostly in the US and China. Every other sector is at the stage of pilots, showroom or reception roles, or demos. In most of those sectors, **non-humanoid precursors** (delivery robots, quadruped inspectors, truck-unloading arms, floor scrubbers, restocking arms) already prove there is demand for robotic physical labour. The sector map does support the claim that humanoids address a far wider task range than industrial arms, cobots or AMRs. But the timing differs sharply by sector: roughly 2026–2030 for factories and warehouses, 2030–2035 for commercial services, and 2035+ for homes and hands-on care.

#### 1.8 Agriculture and food processing

### Inferences
- **Breadth argument (supports Robotify's message).** No previous class of robots touches more than two or three of the 14 sectors above with the same hardware. Industrial arms are confined to fixed cells, AMRs to flat-floor transport, quadrupeds to inspection. Humanoids and mobile manipulators appear in pilots or demos in **all 14 sectors**. The "one platform, many jobs" thesis is therefore directionally supported **by pilots and demos, not yet by scaled deployments.**
- **Where paid work happens in 2026.** Repetitive material handling (totes, parts, bins) in automotive, electronics and contract logistics. Typical tasks are low-dexterity: pick, carry, place, load fixtures.
- **Pattern across sectors.** Wherever a specialised robot can do the job with wheels or a fixed base (floors, inspection, sidewalk delivery, baggage halls), the precursor wins first. Humanoids gain an edge where the task mixes walking, stairs or steps, varied objects and human-designed tools inside one shift.
- **Indicative sector timing** (synthesis of the analyst views in sections 2 and 4 plus the deployment evidence above; this is a judgement, not a sourced forecast):

| Sector | 2026 (now) | ~2030 | 2035+ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Manufacturing (handling, tending, kitting) | Paid pilots and early deployments (US, CN, DE) | Fleets of tens to hundreds per plant at leaders | Mainstream in brownfield plants |
| Warehousing and logistics | Paid RaaS (Digit at GXO) | Scaled tote and box handling; truck work shared with specialised robots | Mainstream |
| Airports (ground handling, baggage) | Trials (JAL Haneda) | Selected hubs with labour shortages | Broad |
| Retail (restocking, backroom) | Precursors plus small pilots | Night restocking pilots at scale | Common |
| Hospitality (housekeeping, F&B) | Demos plus delivery bots | First housekeeping pilots | Partial adoption |

## 2. Size of the opportunity: labour pools, wage bill, shortages and analyst TAMs (global, EU, Poland)

### Takeaway
On the analyst side, the most-cited "blue-sky" numbers come from Morgan Stanley (≈$5 trillion annual market and >1 billion humanoids by 2050) and Citi (≈648 million humanoids by 2050, ~$7 trillion). Goldman Sachs ($38 billion by 2035) and BofA (~1 million annual shipments by 2030) are far more conservative for the next decade. The "total addressable labour" argument rests on the global wage bill, which is about half of world GDP (tens of trillions of USD a year), with physical work a large share of it. For the EU and Poland, labour shortages in construction, care, logistics and hospitality, plus ageing, give a strong demand-side story. **Exact sector headcounts and wage bills for the EU and Poland were not verified in this session.**

### Inferences
- **The investor headline "every physical job can eventually be done by a humanoid" is consistent with the most bullish analyst framing,** e.g. Morgan Stanley (>1 billion units, 75% of US occupations "affected") and Citi (648 million humanoids by 2050). It should be presented as a **long-term (2040–2050) addressable-market thesis**, alongside the conservative near-term numbers (Goldman Sachs $38 billion by 2035; Morgan Stanley about 13 million units by 2035). Otherwise it risks credibility with sophisticated investors.
- **A defensible framing:** "≈half of world GDP is paid to labour; a large share of that is physical work; humanoids are the first robot class designed to address it across sectors rather than one task at a time".
- **For Poland, the demand story rests on three things:**
  1. Shortages in exactly the humanoid target occupations (Barometr zawodów).
  2. Rapidly rising wages, with the minimum wage up over 70% since 2019. **[M-med]**
  3. Low robot density versus Germany and Czechia, so many brownfield plants have no automation to "rebuild around".
- **The counterweight for Poland:** labour costs at about 45% of the EU average push humanoid payback later than in Western Europe.

## 3. Why humanoids specifically (versus specialised robots), and the counter-arguments

### Takeaway
The case for humanoids is **brownfield compatibility plus general-purpose redeployability, plus AI learning from human data.** The world's workplaces, tools and workflows are built for the human body, so a human-shaped robot can in principle step into existing jobs without rebuilding the site, and be retasked across many jobs in one shift. The counter-case is strong: specialised or wheeled robots are cheaper, faster and safer for any single well-defined task. Humanoid dexterity, reliability, battery life and safety certification are not yet at industrial grade. Many impressive demos are teleoperated or tightly scripted.

### Cited Findings
- **Pro arguments (industry consensus framing).**
  - Stairs, doors, aisles, workbenches and hand tools are human-sized.
  - A humanoid can be dropped into brownfield sites. One platform amortises R&D and manufacturing scale across many sectors.
  - Learning from human video and teleoperation data is easiest when the robot's body resembles a human's.
  - Foundation models for robots have emerged quickly: Nvidia Isaac GR00T N1 (Mar 2025), Physical Intelligence π0 / π0.5 (2024–25), Figure Helix (Feb 2025), Google DeepMind Gemini Robotics (Mar 2025). **[M-high]**
  - Sources: Goldman Sachs (link in 2.1); company announcements.
- **Cost trajectory.**
  - Goldman Sachs reports a fall in humanoid BOM cost of about 40% in a year (2023→2024). **[M-high]**
  - BofA projects about $17k per unit by 2030. **[M-med]**
  - Chinese list prices in 2025 were already USD 6–16k for Unitree's small platforms, though these are not industrial-grade. **[M-high]**
- **Counter-argument 1: dexterity and learning.** Rodney Brooks (iRobot co-founder, ex-MIT CSAIL director) argues that today's humanoids will not learn human-level dexterity from video and teleoperation data alone, because they lack touch sensing. He also argues that full-size bipedal humanoids are unsafe to be near (he suggests keeping about 3 m away while they walk), and predicts that successful "humanoids" in 15 years will have wheels and varied hands. **[M-high]** Source: Rodney Brooks, "Why Today's Humanoids Won't Learn Dexterity" (Sep 2025), https://rodneybrooks.com/why-todays-humanoids-wont-learn-dexterity/.
- **Counter-argument 2: safety and standards.**
  - Dynamically stable (legged) robots can fall when power or control fails. No harmonised safety standard yet covers legged mobile manipulators working near people. ISO 25785-1 (industrial mobile robots with actively controlled stability) is under development. **[M-med]**
  - In the EU, the **new Machinery Regulation (EU) 2023/1230** applies from **20 January 2027**. It covers AI-driven, self-evolving behaviour and mobile machinery, and will govern humanoid CE marking. **[M-high]** Source: https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2023/1230/oj.
  - The **EU AI Act** (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689) treats AI safety components of machinery as high-risk. **[M-high]**
- **Counter-argument 3: teleoperation and demo inflation.**
  - Tesla's Optimus robots at the "We, Robot" event (Oct 2024) were largely teleoperated. **[M-high]**
  - 1X NEO relies on remote "expert mode" teleoperation for many home tasks at launch. **[M-high]**
- **Counter-argument 4: historical failures of service humanoids or service robots.** SoftBank Pepper production pause (2020) **[M-high]**; Henn-na Hotel retiring half of its robots (2019) **[M-high]**; DARPA Robotics Challenge falls (2015) **[M-high]**.
- **Counter-argument 5: specialised robots keep winning when the task is well defined.** Examples: Boston Dynamics' own non-humanoid Stretch for truck unloading at DHL; Amazon's 1 million mostly non-humanoid robots; quadrupeds for inspection. **[M-high]** / **[M-med]** (see sections 1.2 and 1.10).
- **Counter-argument 6: bubble risk.** China's NDRC warning (Nov 2025) about more than 150 humanoid makers. **[M-high]**

### Inferences
- **The strongest honest form of Robotify's claim is:** "humanoids are the first robots whose addressable task set is, in principle, the whole human physical job map. Specialised robots will keep winning single tasks, but humanoids win wherever the work is varied, the site is brownfield, or volumes are too small to justify custom automation". For an SME-heavy economy like Poland's, small batches and brownfield sites are the norm, so this framing is particularly relevant.
- **Wheeled-base "humanoids"** (Galbot, AgiBot's wheeled models, Apptronik's wheeled variant) blur the line. The investor message should be "human-like manipulation and form-factor compatibility", not "legs at all costs".
- **EU regulation (Machinery Regulation from Jan 2027, AI Act) is both a barrier and a moat.** An integrator able to certify humanoid cells under EU rules gains an advantage over direct imports. This is an inference relevant to Robotify's positioning.

### Gaps
- **Missing data.** No independent, public benchmark of humanoid productivity (units/hour against a human), uptime, mean time between failures (MTBF) or battery runtime in a real deployment was found. Figure, Agility and UBTech publish only selective metrics.
- **Unverified.** The current status of ISO 25785-1 and any A3 or IEEE humanoid-specific standards in 2026 (an IEEE Humanoid Study Group report was published in 2025 **[M-med]**).

## 4. Realistic sequencing: which sectors come first, and expert timelines

### Takeaway
There is broad consensus across analysts, vendors and sceptics on the order: (1) **structured industrial and logistics** work (2025–2030: material handling, machine tending, tote moving); then (2) **semi-structured commercial services** (2030–2035: retail backrooms, airports, hospital logistics, hotel back-of-house); then (3) **unstructured, human-facing** work (2035+: homes, hands-on elderly care, construction finishing, hospitality front-of-house). Analysts differ mainly on the slope. Morgan Stanley and Goldman Sachs see slow adoption through the early-to-mid 2030s and acceleration later. Vendors (Tesla, Figure, 1X) claim homes and mass production within 1–3 years, which has historically slipped.

### Cited Findings
- **Morgan Stanley.** About 13 million humanoids by 2035, mostly in factories and warehouses, then exponential growth to over 1 billion by 2050. Industrial and commercial uses stay above 90% of the installed base, with households lagging because of cost, safety and regulation. **[V]** — [OODA Loop](https://oodaloop.com/briefs/technology/humanoid-robots-seen-as-5-trillion-global-opportunity-at-morgan-stanley/); [Humanoid.guide](https://humanoid.guide/?p=3205).
- **Goldman Sachs.** Factories (EV, electronics, automotive) first in the mid-to-late 2020s. Consumer and home use only from the 2030s. About 1.4 million units shipped in 2035 in the base case. **[M-high]** (link in 2.1).
- **BofA.** About 1 million shipments per year by 2030, scaling to about 3 billion units in use by 2060. **[M-med]**
- **Vendor timelines (marketing; repeatedly missed):**
  - Tesla: thousands of Optimus units in 2025 (missed) and a million-unit line targeted later. **[M-med]**
  - Figure: home alpha tests from 2025–26. **[M-med]**
  - 1X: home deliveries in 2026, with teleoperation assistance. **[M-high]**
- **Manufacturer plans (more credible because they are tied to capex):**
  - Hyundai: Atlas at Hyundai Motor Group Metaplant America from 2028, assembly tasks around 2030. **[M-med]**
  - UBTech: production ramp to thousands of units per year in 2026–27. **[M-med]**
  - Agility RoboFab: 10,000-unit annual capacity. **[M-high]**
  - Figure BotQ: 12,000 units per year. **[M-med]**
- **Sceptic timeline.** Rodney Brooks expects useful general-purpose humanoids to take well over a decade, and the winning designs to be wheeled with non-human hands. **[M-high]** (link in section 3).
- **Actual 2025 shipments.** Industry trackers (e.g. Omdia, TrendForce, GGII) put global humanoid shipments in 2025 in the low tens of thousands at most, with Chinese vendors (Unitree, AgiBot, UBTech, Leju, Fourier) accounting for the large majority. **[M-med]** Exact figures vary by tracker and were not verified.

### Inferences
- **Recommended sequencing for Robotify's investor narrative** (inference, aligned with the analyst consensus):
  1. **Phase 1, 2026–2028: proof in structured work.** Machine tending, kitting and line-side logistics in Polish automotive, appliance and electronics plants; tote and box handling in logistics parks (Poznań, Łódź, Wrocław, Silesia, Warsaw). Revenue on a robot-hour / RaaS model.
  2. **Phase 2, 2028–2032: semi-structured services.** Retail backrooms and night restocking, airport ground handling (Warsaw Chopin / the planned CPK hub), hospital logistics, hotel back-of-house (laundry, linen, room-service delivery), food processing and packing lines.
  3. **Phase 3, 2032–2035+: unstructured, human-facing work.** Assisted elderly care (fetch, carry, monitoring; patient lifting only once certified), housekeeping, construction finishing, households.
- **Wording.** "Every physical job can *eventually* be done by a humanoid" is a defensible long-horizon claim (2040–2050, Morgan Stanley and Citi scale) if each sector is paired with a phase and with the evidence level (deployment, pilot or demo). The table in section 1 can serve as the investor exhibit.
- **The biggest timing risks:**
  1. Safety certification for legged robots near people (EU Machinery Regulation from 2027; ISO 25785-1).
  2. Dexterity and reliability in unstructured settings.
  3. Unit economics at Polish wage levels.
  4. Hype-cycle correction (China's NDRC warning, missed vendor targets).

### Gaps
- **Expert-survey evidence.** No sourced expert-survey timeline (e.g. a Delphi study) on humanoid adoption by sector was found. The sequencing above is an analyst-consensus synthesis plus inference.
- **Tracker data.** 2025 and H1 2026 global shipment numbers by vendor and sector (Omdia, IDC, TrendForce, Interact Analysis, GGII) need to be verified.
- **Latest views.** Any October 2026 updates to the Morgan Stanley, Goldman Sachs, BofA or Citi forecasts, and IFR World Robotics 2026 commentary on humanoids, are not covered here.
