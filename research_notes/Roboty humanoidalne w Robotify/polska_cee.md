## 1. Labour market: shortages, wages and labour costs, demographics, foreign workers

### Takeaway
Polish manufacturing still has the most vacancies of any sector (24.7k at the end of Q2 2026), but the labour market cooled in 2026. Employment in the enterprise sector is falling (-0.8% y/y in Aug 2026) and wage growth slowed to 5.6% y/y. The structural case for automation still holds: hourly labour costs rose about 9% in 2025, the minimum wage goes to 4,950 PLN in 2027, the working-age population is projected to shrink by 25–40% by 2060, and one in roughly 13 insured workers is foreign (two-thirds Ukrainian).

### Inferences
- Rough labour-cost benchmark for RaaS pricing (my own calculation, not sourced). 9,260 PLN/month ÷ about 168 h ≈ 55 PLN/h gross. Adding employer social charges (I assume about 20%) gives roughly 65–67 PLN/h fully loaded for an average enterprise-sector worker. At the 2027 minimum wage, 32.30 PLN/h gross becomes about 39 PLN/h fully loaded. Production-line and warehouse roles probably sit between these two figures. A cobot or humanoid "robot-hour" priced below about 40–60 PLN/h would undercut a manned shift, before counting the cost of shift premiums, absenteeism and recruitment.
- The 2026 cooling (falling employment, slower wage growth, lower hiring intentions) means pure "we can't find people" messaging is weaker than in 2021–2023. The pitch should stress cost predictability, flexible capacity, and the demographic outlook as the risk to hedge.
- Reliance on Ukrainian labour (857k insured) is a concentration risk. Any change in their legal status or a post-war return home would hit logistics and manufacturing first. This is a strong argument to use with mid-sized warehouse operators.
- The Eurostat 2025 hourly cost for Poland (about €19, unverified) is about 55% of the EU average and above HU (€15.2) and RO (€13.6). For a payback-based sales pitch, the automation case is therefore stronger in Poland than in RO or HU, and much stronger in DACH.

## 2. Robotisation level: density, installations, cobots, humanoids, the robotics ecosystem in Poland and CEE

### Takeaway
Poland is the largest CEE robot market by annual installations, about 2,400 in 2025 (+2%, after three years of decline from a peak of over 3,500 in 2021). Its robot density, however, is only about half of Czechia's or Slovakia's and well under half of the EU-27 average of 231 per 10,000 manufacturing employees. Only about 4% of Polish SMEs use industrial robots, compared with 18% of large firms. I found no confirmed humanoid deployment in a Polish factory or warehouse as of October 2026. The nearest CEE industrial humanoid case is Mercedes-Benz Kecskemét (HU) with Apptronik Apollo. In Poland, humanoids so far appear in demos, PR and retail (Unitree G1 at about 100k PLN).

- The density gap (Poland about 102 against CZ 216, SK 210, HU 172 and an EU average of 231) and the 4% SME adoption rate show that the addressable "first robot" market in Polish SMEs is large but largely untouched. That favours a low-entry-barrier rental model.
- Poland's installation stagnation coexisted with a generous tax relief. This suggests capex aversion and integration complexity, not tax treatment, are the binding constraints for SMEs, which is the problem RaaS addresses.
- Humanoids in Poland are at the "PR and demo" stage. A Robotify humanoid offer in 2027–2028 would probably be among the first commercial industrial humanoid services in Poland. That is a first-mover advantage, but there is no local reference case. Mercedes Kecskemét is the nearest CEE proof point.
- The security controversy around the Unitree G1 at a naval base means Chinese-made humanoids (Unitree, Agibot, UBTech) may face procurement objections in defence-adjacent, critical-infrastructure and some automotive clients. Offering an EU or US option (NEURA, Agility, Apptronik, Humanoid) alongside a cheap Chinese option may be needed.
- Retail pricing of about 100k PLN for a G1 (about $13.5k at source plus import costs and VAT) suggests a 2–3x markup in Polish retail. A rental provider buying direct could undercut retail significantly.

## 3. Funding and incentives (Poland, CEE) and how a RaaS provider or its clients can use them

### Takeaway
The main Polish instrument is the **ulga na robotyzację** (CIT/PIT 50% additional deduction). It formally expires at the end of the tax year starting in 2026. A government draft (**UD461**, entered on the legislative list on 27 Aug 2026) would extend it by 10 years to tax years starting in 2036, at the same 50% rate, but it was not yet law as of mid-October 2026. EU grant money for SME robotisation in 2026 is limited: KPO's robotisation measure (A2.1.1) was for large firms only, FEPW's "Automatyzacja i robotyzacja w MŚP" is limited to eastern Poland, and FENG Ścieżka SMART is heavily oversubscribed (515%) and R&D-oriented. BGK guarantees (Biznesmax Plus) support bank loans, but I found no evidence they cover leasing or rental.

### Inferences
- **Ulga timing:** until UD461 is passed and signed, 2026 is formally the last year of the relief. Clients deciding before year-end 2026 may rush purchases, which could temporarily favour buying over renting. If the extension passes, the relief stays a 10-year feature of the market. If the extension slips, RaaS gains relative appeal.
- **Who can use the ulga in a RaaS model** (needs a tax adviser's confirmation). The relief is tied to costs the taxpayer incurs to acquire robots and related items, with leasing payments a disputed area. A Robotify client paying a rental or service fee is probably not claiming the relief on that fee. Robotify, as owner of the robots, would claim it only if its own use of the robots qualifies under the statute. This is a key structuring question: options include a finance-lease wrapper with a leasing partner, or a "rent-to-own" path that keeps clients eligible.
- Grants (FEPW, SMART) usually finance the beneficiary's own fixed assets or services within a project. A RaaS fee may be eligible as a service or operating cost in some calls but not as capex. Each call's kryteria and katalog kosztów kwalifikowanych must be checked. Robotify could position itself as the "dostawca usługi" (service provider) inside clients' grant projects.
- With KPO SME money gone and SMART oversubscribed five times, most target clients will not get grants. The ulga (where applicable) and the absence of capex will be the stronger levers.

## 5. Attitudes and barriers: SME willingness, financing, unions and workers, safety and inspection

### Takeaway
Polish SMEs say automation matters: 76–77% in PIE's late-2023 survey, and about a third planned robotisation in the 2019 UR barometer, with cobots favoured by smaller firms. Actual adoption is still only about 4% of SMEs. Installations stagnated despite the 50% tax relief, which suggests capex, skills and integration barriers matter more than tax. The regulatory baseline changes on **20 Jan 2027**, when the EU Machinery Regulation 2023/1230 replaces the Machinery Directive and brings AI and cybersecurity requirements. Anyone who substantially modifies a machine counts as its manufacturer, which is relevant to RaaS reconfigurations. Security sensitivity around Chinese humanoids is now visible in Poland.

### Cited Findings
**Willingness and plans**
- PIE survey (end of 2023): 76% of firms think robotisation and automation are important for keeping a competitive advantage, 77% among SMEs. PIE expects robotisation to advance as labour costs rise. — [PIE, Jul 2024](https://pie.net.pl/wp-content/uploads/2024/07/2024-07-03_Polski-przemysl-potrzebuje-wiecej-robotow.pdf); [XYZ](https://xyz.pl/dzieje-sie/pie-w-2024-r-roboty-przemyslowe-mialo-18-proc-duzych-firm-i-tylko-4-proc-msp-3802)

### Inferences
- The SME barrier is "first robot" risk: capex, lack of in-house integration skills, and uncertainty about payback. A rental model with a pilot period, a robot-hour price and Robotify-owned integration risk targets exactly the gap between 77% stated importance and 4% adoption.
- **Regulatory implication for Robotify:** robots and cells put into service after 20 Jan 2027 must meet 2023/1230. If Robotify reconfigures a rented cobot cell for a new client and process, it may count as a "manufacturer" of a new or substantially modified machine (Art. 18), with CE, risk-assessment and documentation duties. Standardised, pre-certified cell templates would reduce that burden. Humanoids with AI and adaptive behaviour will draw extra scrutiny under the AI-related provisions and, separately, the AI Act. This needs legal confirmation.
- Using Chinese humanoids in sensitive clients (defence-adjacent, energy, some automotive Tier-1s) may trigger security objections. Data-locality and cybersecurity guarantees, and the CRA from 2027, should be part of the offer.
